# The Bible: Textual Transmission and Historical Evidence

The "Bible" is not one book. The transmission question must be answered
separately for the **Hebrew Bible / Old Testament** and the **Greek New
Testament**, because their evidence is very different.

---

## Part A — The Hebrew Bible / Old Testament

### The evidence base

- **Masoretic Text (MT):** the standardized Hebrew tradition fixed by the
  Masoretes (c. 7th–10th c. CE). Key codices: Aleppo Codex (c. 920 CE),
  Leningrad Codex (1008 CE — the basis of printed Hebrew Bibles).
- **Dead Sea Scrolls (DSS):** c. 250 BCE – 68 CE, found at Qumran. They
  push physical evidence back **roughly a thousand years** earlier than the
  great Masoretic codices.
- **Septuagint (LXX):** Greek translation of the Hebrew, begun c. 3rd c.
  BCE.
- **Samaritan Pentateuch (SP):** an independent Torah tradition.

### What the evidence shows

- **Strong stability where it can be tested.** The Great Isaiah Scroll
  (1QIsaᵃ) is ~1,000 years older than the medieval MT yet is
  *substantially* the same text; differences are overwhelmingly spelling,
  word order, and minor wording.
- **But genuine textual pluriformity existed before standardization.** At
  Qumran the OT circulated in multiple text-types. The LXX of **Jeremiah**
  is ~1/7 shorter and arranged differently than the MT; **1–2 Samuel**
  shows substantial LXX/Qumran variation; Psalm numbering and some
  passages differ. The "one stable Hebrew text" only becomes the dominant
  reality *after* roughly the 1st–2nd c. CE.
- **Composition vs. transmission.** Mainstream critical scholarship holds
  that the Pentateuch and many OT books are **composite**, edited from
  multiple sources/traditions over centuries (the broad legacy of the
  Documentary Hypothesis and its successors). This is a *compositional*
  issue, distinct from copying fidelity.

**Net for the OT:** transmission from the standardization era onward is
demonstrably conservative; *before* standardization the text was plural,
and the deeper academic debate is about composition and editing, not about
medieval scribes corrupting a once-pristine text.

## Part B — The Greek New Testament

### The evidence base (unmatched in antiquity)

- **~5,800+ Greek manuscripts**, plus ~10,000+ Latin and thousands in
  other versions, plus tens of thousands of patristic quotations.
- **Earliest fragments very early:** P52 (John, c. 125–175 CE); major
  papyri (P45, P46, P66, P75) from the late 2nd–3rd c.; great codices
  Sinaiticus and Vaticanus 4th c.
- No other work of antiquity is even close in manuscript volume or in the
  gap between composition and earliest copies.

### The variant problem — stated honestly

- There are an estimated **~400,000 textual variants** — *more variants
  than words in the NT*. This is the apologetic Muslim camp's strongest
  factual point and must not be minimized.
- **However**, the overwhelming majority are: spelling, word order
  (Greek is inflected, so order rarely changes meaning), nonsense
  scribal slips, and synonyms. The number is large mainly *because the
  manuscript pool is huge*.
- **Variants that are both meaningful and viable** are a small fraction.
  The few theologically weighty ones are well known and *openly printed in
  modern Bibles' footnotes*:
  - **Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8)** — an explicit Trinity proof-text;
    **not original**, a late Latin insertion. Modern scholarship and
    modern Bibles reject it.
  - **Longer ending of Mark (16:9–20)** and **the woman caught in
    adultery (John 7:53–8:11)** — almost certainly **not original** to
    those Gospels; flagged in every critical edition.
  - **Luke 22:43–44**, **1 Tim 3:16**, **John 1:18** — text-critically
    contested.

### The scholarly dispute

- **Bart Ehrman** (*Misquoting Jesus*; *The Orthodox Corruption of
  Scripture*) argues some variants are theologically motivated ("orthodox
  corruptions") and that certainty about the exact original wording is
  limited.
- **Daniel Wallace, Bruce Metzger, Larry Hurtado** counter that the sheer
  manuscript abundance lets scholars *reconstruct* the early text with high
  confidence, and that — as Ehrman himself concedes in interviews — **no
  cardinal Christian doctrine stands or falls on a textually disputed
  passage** (the doctrines are taught in many undisputed passages).
- Both camps agree on the *data*; they disagree on how much *interpretive*
  significance to assign it.

## Arbiter verdict — Bible transmission

**Present.**
- *Defense:* unparalleled manuscript evidence, early attestation, a
  reconstructable early text, and full scholarly transparency about the
  disputed passages.
- *Prosecution:* hundreds of thousands of variants, a few doctrinally
  sensitive ones, no autographs, and a composite/edited OT.

**Weigh.**
- The claim "the NT message was textually fabricated or wholesale altered"
  is **not supported** by the manuscript evidence — it proves the opposite
  (we can trace and isolate changes precisely *because* we have so many
  copies).
- The claim "the transmitted text is not letter-perfect and a few
  theologically loaded passages are demonstrably later" is **well
  supported and conceded by mainstream scholarship**.
- The OT's real academic vulnerability is **composition and pre-1st-century
  pluriformity**, not medieval copying.

**Judge.**
On the narrow historical question — *can the early text be recovered, and
was the core message transmitted intact?* — the evidence favors the
**reliability** side: the NT is the best-attested ancient text and its
substantive content is recoverable and stable. On the **purity** claim —
*the text we hold is identical to the autographs in every word* — the
evidence favors the **critics**: it demonstrably is not, and Christianity's
own scholars say so in print. The honest position is therefore split by
question, and any argument that uses NT variants to imply *invention* of
the message **proves too much and fails** (Methodology §4).
