# Methodology: Evidentiary Standards and Logical Rules

To judge fairly, the rules must be fixed *before* the evidence is examined
and applied identically to both scriptures.

## 1. What counts as evidence

In descending order of weight:

1. **Physical manuscripts**, dated by paleography and radiocarbon.
2. **Quotations and references** in datable external sources (other
   authors, lectionaries, inscriptions, translations).
3. **The internal testimony** of the tradition (how the community says its
   text was transmitted) — useful, but it is a *claim* about history, not
   automatically history.
4. **Apologetic argument** — clarifies positions but is **not** evidence in
   itself.

## 2. Defining "corruption" precisely

The word *corruption* is used loosely in debate. This dossier distinguishes:

| Type | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Transmission corruption** | The text today differs materially from what was first written | Manuscript comparison across time |
| **Compositional concern** | The "original" itself is composite, anonymous, or late | Source criticism, dating |
| **Interpretive corruption** (*tahrif al-maʿna*) | The wording is intact but meaning is distorted in reading | Exegetical, not textual |
| **Wholesale fabrication** | The text was invented and back-projected | Extraordinary claim; needs extraordinary evidence |

A precise debate keeps these separate. Most apologetic confusion comes
from sliding between them — e.g., citing trivial spelling variants
(transmission) to argue the message was invented (fabrication).

## 3. The symmetry principle

Any argument is run **in both directions**:

- "The Bible has hundreds of thousands of manuscript variants" → then the
  Qur'an's variant readings (*qiraʾat*) and the Sanaa palimpsest must be
  weighed by the *same* yardstick.
- "The Qur'an was standardized by burning rival codices under ʿUthman" →
  then the church's selection and copying of the New Testament canon must
  be weighed by the *same* yardstick.

Selective skepticism — strict with the rival text, lenient with one's own
— is the single most common fallacy in this field and is flagged wherever
it appears.

## 4. Logical rules of engagement

- **The burden of proof lies on the claimant.** "X is corrupt" requires
  evidence *for* corruption, not merely the absence of proof against it.
- **Quantity ≠ significance.** 400,000 NT variants across 5,800+ Greek
  manuscripts is mostly an artifact of having *many* manuscripts; the
  metric that matters is whether variants change meaning or doctrine.
- **Absence of early manuscripts ≠ proof of change.** It lowers certainty;
  it does not by itself prove corruption.
- **An argument that proves too much fails.** A standard that declares the
  Bible "corrupt" but, applied evenly, would equally indict the Qur'an (or
  all ancient literature) is not a usable standard.
- **Genetic fallacy.** A doctrine's late *formalization* (e.g., a creed
  ratified at a council) does not by itself prove the underlying idea was
  absent earlier — that must be shown from the sources.
- **Equivocation on "the Bible/Qur'an."** Specify: Hebrew OT? Greek NT?
  Which textual tradition? Which *qiraʾa*? Vague nouns hide weak arguments.

## 5. Limits of the arbiter

History and textual criticism can answer *what the text says* and *how it
got here*. They **cannot** answer *whether its theology is true*. When a
section's question becomes "is God triune or absolutely one?", the arbiter
rules only on (a) which view each scripture's own text better supports and
(b) which view is internally coherent — **not** on which is divinely
correct.
